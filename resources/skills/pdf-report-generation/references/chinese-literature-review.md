# Chinese literature review: evidence before typesetting

Use this path for a Chinese-language review, especially when the primary evidence is published in Chinese. Decide whether the deliverable is a narrative review, a scoping review, or a systematic review before searching. Do not borrow systematic-review labels, screening diagrams, or certainty grades without the corresponding protocol and records.

## Build a bilingual evidence base

Search Chinese and English terms for the same concepts, including site names, alternate transliterations, period names, and regional boundaries. For archaeology and other locally documented fields, seek excavation reports, catalogues, journal articles, and monographs from authoritative Chinese institutions alongside international indexed papers. Record the source, exact query, date, sort order, pagination, access restrictions, and deduplication rule. If a source is inaccessible, name the missing coverage and narrow the report's scope; an English-only index cannot support a comprehensive claim about Chinese primary evidence.

Save a machine-readable search snapshot with every candidate's stable identifier, complete authors, title, year, venue, language, source URL/DOI, retrieval status, inclusion decision, and exclusion reason. Preserve the original query results or export when permitted. A count such as “167 screened, 33 included” must be reproducible from this table, not reconstructed from memory or a live Notebook variable.

## Verify the bibliography and claims

Resolve each DOI or stable identifier against publisher, bibliographic, or institutional records. Compare **all** authors in order, year, title, venue, volume/pages when available, and correction or retraction status. Do not generate a final citation from a search preview that returns only the first few authors. A valid DOI can still be attached to an incorrect author list or an unsupported claim.

For every headline claim, inspect the source's relevant page, table, figure, or methods/results passage. In the claim–evidence ledger, record site or population, material or sample, dating method and uncertainty, observed result, competing interpretation, and the strongest conclusion the source permits. Mark abstract-only access explicitly. Keep a single-site observation at single-site scope unless independent regional evidence justifies widening it. Separate archaeological observation from linguistic, genetic, or historical inference.

## Write the synthesis

Organize results by research question or dispute rather than one paragraph per cited paper. Use a compact comparison table showing evidence type, site/time, method, finding, limits, and competing explanation. Include negative or contradictory findings when they change the conclusion. A timeline, site-by-evidence matrix, or self-drawn analytical figure can help when its values and sources are auditable; a decorative or invented map does not.

Use natural Chinese scholarly prose and consistent Chinese/English terminology. Keep complete source identifiers and DOI or stable links in the reference list. Before calling a report submission-ready, verify coverage of the field's primary literature, the entire bibliography, all central claims, target-journal requirements, author/affiliation responsibility, and figure rights. If those conditions are unmet, label it an exploratory or draft review and state exactly what remains.
